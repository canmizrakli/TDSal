----------------------- REVIEW 1 ---------------------

SUBMISSION: 5
TITLE: TDSal: A Task-Based Top-Down Saliency Prediction Model

----------- Originality and Innovation -----------
SCORE: 3 (fair)
----- TEXT:
The paper correctly identifies that most saliency models focus on free-viewing (bottom-up) while real-world attention is task-guided.
----------- Significance and Advancement Over the State of the Art -----------
SCORE: 4 (good)
----- TEXT:
The motivation for language-conditioned, top-down saliency is convincing and practically relevant for HRI, autonomous driving, and assistive systems.
----------- Technical Soundness and Reproducibility -----------
SCORE: 4 (good)
----- TEXT:
Unlike existing methods that use full vision–language transformers or diffusion models, TDSal uses a shallow fusion-only transformer and a pretrained detection backbone. This is a clean, effective, and under-explored design for task-driven saliency.
----------- Experimental Evaluation and Analysis; Quality of Visual Results -----------
SCORE: 3 (fair)
----- TEXT:
Comprehensive evaluation using 5 standard saliency metrics (CC, KLDiv, SIM, NSS, AUC–Borji),and Qualitative results clearly demonstrate task-dependent attention shifts
----------- Quality of of Language and Presentation -----------
SCORE: 4 (good)
----- TEXT:
The manuscript is not well-writing
----------- Overall evaluation -----------
SCORE: 1 (weak accept)
----- TEXT:
In this paper, the author proposes the TDSal framework, a lightweight vision–language paradigm for task-driven top-down saliency prediction that integrates multi-scale visual features with task embeddings via a shallow transformer fusion module. Overall, the work is clearly motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated. Moreover, it emphasizes an important gap in goal-directed attention modeling and achieves strong results on a task-conditioned eye-tracking dataset. However, several issues remain to be addressed.

1. Weak experimental comparison. Specifically, baseline comparisons in the experiments are ambiguous. Direct comparison to free-viewing models is unfair and biased; task-driven saliency baselines should be used instead. Therefore, task/text-conditioned saliency models should be included for a complete comparison. Additionally, an efficiency analysis is missing: the paper claims to be lightweight but does not provide complexity measurements such as number of parameters, inference speed, or FLOPs; these metrics should be reported.

2. Ambiguous description of the architecture. For example, it is unclear how many multiscale YOLO features are fused before the FPM. The authors should clarify the feature fusion process in the backbone.

3. Limited dataset scale and diversity. As noted in the limitations, using only 1,968 samples across four fixation tasks is insufficient to validate the framework. There is no evaluation on unseen tasks, new domains, or larger datasets. The authors should discuss generalization to open-ended or unseen tasks and ideally evaluate on larger or more diverse data.

In light of the significance of the proposed method, the reviewer believes this manuscript can be accepted after adequate revision.

----------------------- REVIEW 3 ---------------------

SUBMISSION: 5
TITLE: TDSal: A Task-Based Top-Down Saliency Prediction Model

----------- Originality and Innovation -----------
SCORE: 4 (good)
----- TEXT:
The paper introduces a task-conditioned saliency model (TDSal) that integrates natural-language task descriptions into saliency prediction using a lightweight vision–language fusion design. While task-driven saliency and vision–language fusion are not new concepts, the specific combination of a YOLO-based multiscale backbone, Sentence-BERT embeddings, and a shallow transformer for fusion is a reasonably novel architecture choice. The emphasis on lightweight fusion rather than heavy multimodal architectures is a practical contribution. However, the core components themselves (YOLO, SBERT, transformer fusion) are well-established, and the innovation lies more in integration than in fundamentally new methodology.
----------- Significance and Advancement Over the State of the Art -----------
SCORE: 3 (fair)
----- TEXT:
The paper provides a clear and well-structured positioning within the existing literature, explicitly distinguishing its task-driven setting from conventional free-viewing saliency benchmarks.

Its contributions align well with current research directions, particularly in vision–language modeling, explainable AI, and human-centered attention analysis. However, the degree of advancement over the state of the art is somewhat constrained. This is primarily due to the lack of direct comparisons on shared benchmarks, the use of a relatively small and task-specific dataset, and the absence of evaluations against strong, recent multimodal baselines under consistent experimental conditions.

Overall, the authors are transparent in discussing these limitations. This strengthens the paper’s credibility, even as it underscores that the contribution is more incremental than transformative.
----------- Technical Soundness and Reproducibility -----------
SCORE: 3 (fair)
----- TEXT:
The architecture is described in a coherent and interpretable manner, while the training procedure and loss formulation are specified with sufficient detail. Dataset splits, preprocessing steps, and augmentation strategies are documented carefully, including the use of a fixed random seed, which supports reproducibility.
A key strength of the work is its modular design, with a clear explanation of why these architectural choices were made. The inclusion of a detailed evaluation further strengthens the technical evaluation by providing insight into the role of individual design components.
That said, some aspects could be better justified. Additionally, code is not available at submission time, which limits immediate reproducibility, and the use of a relatively uncommon dataset makes independent comparison more difficult.
Overall, the paper makes a solid effort toward reproducibility, but falls short of being fully complete in this regard.
----------- Experimental Evaluation and Analysis; Quality of Visual Results -----------
SCORE: 3 (fair)
----- TEXT:
The evaluation consists of standard saliency metrics (e.g., CC, NSS, AUC-Borji) and qualitative visualizations. But there is no direct comparison with competing methods on the same dataset, and the benchmark results are explicitly non-comparable due to differences in datasets and evaluation protocols. In addition, the dataset size is relatively small (1,968 samples), which limits the statistical strength of the conclusions, and no statistical significance testing is reported. The evaluation also lacks diversity in test scenarios.

While the qualitative results are generally reasonable, the analysis does not assess model robustness in challenging conditions.
----------- Quality of of Language and Presentation -----------
SCORE: 4 (good)
----- TEXT:
The paper is very well written, with a clear structure and a logical flow that makes the content easy to follow. Key concepts and motivations are explained effectively, and the figures and tables are both informative and well integrated into the narrative. The writing is careful and precise, reflecting strong scientific communication throughout.

Notably, the authors are also transparent in discussing limitations and the non-comparability of benchmarks, which demonstrates a commendable level of academic rigor and integrity.
----------- Overall evaluation -----------
SCORE: 0 (borderline paper)
----- TEXT:
This paper presents a well-executed approach to task-driven saliency prediction, with a clear architectural design and consistent empirical results.

The proposed lightweight vision–language fusion framework is practically relevant and well aligned with current research directions in saliency modeling. However, the main limitations include a lack of strong and comprehensive benchmarking against prior work, limited experimental validation, and relatively limited methodological novelty.

Despite these weaknesses, the paper remains coherent and technically sound, offering useful insights into task-conditioned attention modeling.
